# A design leader’s field guide to AI

Where should design invest in AI, and what would make that investment worthwhile? This directory pairs people to learn from with work you can inspect. The [nine company case studies](ai-design-company-cases.md) give the evidence behind changes in speed, quality, and customer behavior.

- **[Leading in the age of AI](#leading-in-the-age-of-ai)** — Choose investments, shape roles, and agree on what teams share.
- **[Defining and measuring impact](#defining-and-measuring-impact)** — Decide what success means and what evidence would justify scaling.
- **[Craft and quality](#craft-and-quality)** — Preserve design intent, originality, and consistency as output grows.
- **[Customer value and trust](#customer-value-and-trust)** — Build experiences people can use, verify, and control.

## Leading in the age of AI

How should we choose what to build, divide the work, and spend the time AI gives back? The goal is a team that reaches better decisions and knows who owns them.

### Which bets deserve the team’s time?

- **[Rachel Kobetz — deciding what design leadership owns](https://kobewan.substack.com/p/dx-perspective-whats-worth-making)**  
  Kobetz asks what happens when a team can build an idea before anyone agrees it is worth making. She connects portfolio choices, consistency across tools, and the new responsibility of designing for agents that act on a customer’s behalf.

- **[Jasmine and Tanner Christensen — explaining design’s contribution to the business](https://newlayerpodcast.com/episodes/2026/11/where-does-design-go-from-here/)**  
  The New Layer hosts revisit Mills Baker’s [Designer Duds](https://medium.com/@millsbaker/designer-duds-f59c964513ef) to ask where design contributes most when anyone can generate another direction. Their discussion is a useful starting point for leaders deciding whether to spend the time saved on more variants or a more ambitious problem.

- **[Filip Kaliszan, Tamar Yehoshua, and Maddi Holman — funding a second product bet](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n073LFOjcwM)**  
  Leaders from Verkada, Atlassian, and Daring Ventures describe how customer demand pulled companies into adjacent markets. Their examples help distinguish an interesting prototype from an opportunity that warrants people, funding, and sustained attention.

### Who should build, critique, and approve the work?

- **[Anthropic — bringing designers into production in their first week](https://designerfund.substack.com/p/ai-design-anthropic)**  
  Joel Lewenstein, Meaghan Choi, Nate Parrott, Kyle Turman, and Matt Gallivan describe designers contributing code and running small critiques up to four times a week. The [team’s account](https://stateofaidesign.com/cases/anthropic) explains the change in practice. Its 3–5× estimate for design speed is illustrative.

- **[Jenny Wen — carrying a product vision into implementation](https://jennywen.ca/)**  
  Wen helped set the direction for Cowork, tested it through internal prototypes, and contributed code around launch. Her [interview on design leadership](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-design-process-is-dead) gives a concrete view of how a design lead can stay close to implementation while setting direction for others.

- **[Adyen — reviewing real components while a UI is being built](https://www.figma.com/customers/how-adyen-is-rebuilding-design/)**  
  Hong Zou brought product, design, and engineering together on roadmap problems. Hanbin Chang’s team connected Figma frames to real components and tokens so designers and engineers could review working interfaces together. Adyen reports that selected builds fell from two or three days to about two hours.

- **[trivago — connecting UX writing and localization](https://tech.trivago.com/post/2026-10-02-from-tools-to-teammates-how-ai-empowered-our-ux-team-to-level-up)**  
  The UX team linked drafting, translation keys, and translation requests so copy could move through review without manual handoffs. It reports 75% shorter writing turnaround and 41% higher output. The team says quality scores stayed high, although it did not publish the scoring rubric.

### What should embedded teams share?

- **[Stuart Frisby — giving teams common rules across different tools](https://www.designleadershipmemos.com/p/x-is-dead-is-dead)**  
  Frisby starts from a familiar leadership problem. Designers can choose different tools, but customers still encounter one product. He identifies the product context, components, and decision rules that need a common home as individual workflows diverge.

- **[Ramiro Galan and Adam Cutler — co-designing IBM’s AI standard](https://pixelkings.com/work/ibm-c4ai/)**  
  The [Carbon for AI leads](https://juan.design/work/carbon-for-ai/) worked with 12 early-adopter teams. One [shared component](https://www.carbondesignsystem.com/building-blocks/foundations/carbon-for-ai) lets a customer open an explanation beside an AI-generated field. The case shows how a central team can turn a recurring local need into a standard others will adopt.

- **[Julien Clément and Antoine Deshoux — sharing specialist agents at Carrefour](https://www.figma.com/customers/how-carrefour-leveraged-ai-to-drive-design-delivery/)**  
  Carrefour gave embedded teams a way to propose, test, and publish agents for recurring design tasks. More than 50 agents were approved. The team reported that 80% of designers used Figma Make weekly, a measure of uptake leaders can compare with later outcome data.

## Defining and measuring impact

What improved, for whom, and compared with what? The goal is to decide whether an AI workflow merits further investment using evidence that covers the work from brief to delivery.

### What would count as a better result?

- **[Procter & Gamble — testing whether faster ideas are also better ideas](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33641/w33641.pdf)**  
  In a randomized workshop with 776 professionals, individuals using AI spent 16.4% less time on product proposals than unaided individuals. Experts who did not know how the proposals were produced rated them 0.37 standard deviations higher. The experiment measured concept quality before commercialization.

- **[Intuit TurboTax — testing whether customers respond to faster creative work](https://business.adobe.com/blog/real-time-insights-and-on-demand-creative-for-turbotax)**  
  The first pilot missed its click-through target. In the second, teams used Adobe GenStudio to create and refresh ads. Turnaround went from up to two weeks to as little as four hours, and the treatment ads earned 38% higher click-through than controls.

- **[Figma and MeasuringU — establishing a baseline for prototyping time](https://www.figma.com/blog/measuring-time-savings-from-figma-make/)**  
  A randomized study assigned 100 designers and product managers to three interface tasks. Among those who completed the tasks, participants with access to Figma Make took about 20% less time. Output quality remained an open question for a separate evaluation.

### What does the headline metric leave out?

- **[Stephanie Nyairo — measuring the work between a draft and a deliverable](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/making-generative-ai-workable-at-work-accountability-localization-and-representation-in-kenyan-and-nigerian-small-businesses/)**  
  The Nairobi-based [product designer](https://www.stephanienyairo.com/) and colleagues interviewed 27 workers at 21 businesses in Kenya and Nigeria. People described checking claims, recovering sources, and repairing local language before sending AI-assisted work to clients. Timing the first draft would miss that labor.

- **[Nitin Nohria and Hélène Hannecart — finding the bottleneck after delegation](https://hbr.org/2026/11/the-hidden-costs-of-delegating-to-ai)**  
  Across four accounting firms using AI for tax preparation, faster individual tasks created new demands on reviewers and changed how junior staff learned. The HBR article has a public summary. Its lesson for design operations is to track whose workload grows when someone else’s shrinks.

- **[Ethan Mollick — checking whether an evaluation rewards the right thing](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/fable-52-and-jagged-agi)**  
  Mollick’s agents built virtual worlds and devised a scoring system to guide their revisions. As the agents optimized that score, the author still had to judge whether the experience was enjoyable. It’s a concrete example to use when choosing a quality metric.

## Craft and quality

How do we protect consistency, taste, and originality as output grows? The goal is to give makers precise control and reviewers a repeatable way to recognize work worth shipping.

### Will the output hold up in production?

- **[Jordan Singer — generating with the components a team actually owns](https://ibuildmyideas.substack.com/p/a-tricycle-for-our-minds)**  
  Singer demonstrated a GPT-3 Figma plugin in 2020. His team at Diagram later built [Genius](https://www.figma.com/blog/the-future-of-design-systems-is-automated/), which could inspect a design file and suggest existing components. It offers a practical test for generated UI. Does the screen reference the real component and its behavior?

- **[David Sward, Marianne Ashton-Booth, and Nicolas Chatelain — scaling design review](https://uxdesign.cc/what-ai-has-changed-in-design-systems-and-what-is-still-up-to-us-d4e8e8f4ceb2)**  
  These interviews with Siemens’ design chief, ITV’s head of product design, and an independent design-system specialist examine who approves new patterns. The [Siemens case](https://radar.zurb.com/article/siemens-design-chief-my-biggest-problem-is-trying-to-slow-people-down-with-ai) makes the pressure clear. Generating variants takes seconds, while reviewing consequences across products takes judgment.

- **[Graphical — packaging a design system for a coding agent](https://www.graphicalui.com/)**  
  Graphical exports tokens, React components, and a `GUI.md` guide. Inspect how it packages states, motion, and rules alongside the visual theme. Those details determine whether a new screen behaves like the rest of the product.

- **[Unilever — protecting packaging accuracy in generated campaign images](https://www.unilever.com/news/press-and-media/press-releases/2025/unilever-reinvents-product-shoots-with-digital-twins-and-ai/)**  
  Unilever reused approved 3D models of its products while generating new scenes. For TRESemmé Thailand, it reports twice the production speed, 87% lower content costs, and a 5% increase in purchase intent. The published commercial measure was customers’ stated intent.

### How do we judge the idea as well as the execution?

- **[Jason Wu and Amanda Swearngin — teaching a UI generator through critique](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/designer-feedback)**  
  Their team collected roughly 1,500 annotations from 21 designers. In its evaluation, models trained using detailed designer feedback outperformed the ranking baseline. The method gives a reviewer a way to show exactly which element needs to change.

- **[Boston Consulting Group — separating the quality of a pitch from the originality of its idea](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838)**  
  On a footwear brief, consultants using GPT-4 without additional training reached the final task 27.6% faster. Graders rated overall submissions 29.9% higher, while footwear specialists rated the ideas 11.7% higher for creativity. The choice of reviewer and scoring question mattered.

- **[Dylan Field and David Friedberg — making a point of view visible in the work](https://www.figma.com/blog/in-conversation-dylan-field-and-david-friedberg/)**  
  In their Figma conversation, Field describes using an AI-generated starting point to discover which choices he wants to change. It suggests a useful critique question. Which decisions in this prototype express something distinctive about this product?

- **[Patrick Neeman — bringing a model’s worst answer into critique](https://uxdesign.cc/ai-is-the-new-old-design-material-0e7f83540c9a)**  
  Neeman treats repeated model outputs as material a designer can examine. Run the same request several times, bring a poor result to review, and inspect what the interface lets the customer do next. A handpicked screen can hide that part of the experience.

### Where can a maker exercise finer control?

- **[Rebecca Fiebrink — teaching an instrument by playing it](https://nime.org/proc/nime2009_fiebrink/)**  
  With Dan Trueman and Perry Cook, [Fiebrink](https://www.arts.ac.uk/research/ual-staff-researchers/rebecca-fiebrink) developed Wekinator so musicians could demonstrate a gesture, hear the sound it controlled, and adjust the training examples immediately. The musician shapes the model through the same movements used in performance.

- **[Bjørn Karmann — giving model settings physical controls](https://bjoernkarmann.dk/project/objectifier)**  
  Karmann used Wekinator in an early prototype for Objectifier, which let someone teach a radio to respond to a dance. His later camera, [Paragraphica](https://bjoernkarmann.dk/project/paragraphica), has a dial that changes how far around it the system gathers information for a generated image.

- **[Sougwen Chung — keeping authorship visible in human–machine collaboration](https://sougwen.com/work/memory-drawing-operations)**  
  For MEMORY, Chung trained a robotic drawing partner on 20 years of their own work. Later joint drawings entered the archive. Chung’s [V&A interview](https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/digital/the-algorithmic-gesture-sougwen-chungs-memory) follows the creative decisions that remain with the artist, including when a performance is finished.

- **[Shan Carter and Michael Nielsen — inventing a control through examples](https://distill.pub/2017/aia/)**  
  In their interactive essay, readers choose serif and sans-serif fonts, then use a slider the model learned from those examples. It shows how a creative tool can let a designer define a visual quality that would be hard to specify in words.

## Customer value and trust

What does AI help a customer do, and what should stay under their control? The goal is an experience customers can use, question, and correct when the stakes matter.

### What becomes possible for the customer?

- **[Mitsubishi Electric — letting clients revise a prototype in the meeting](https://www.figma.com/customers/mitsubishi-electric-corporation/)**  
  On one app project, the team cut prototype development from two weeks to one day and ran 12 client and internal demos, up from three. The validation period fell from ten weeks to six. The change gave clients more chances to correct the team’s assumptions.

- **[Duolingo — expanding access to listening practice](https://blog.duolingo.com/scaling-duoradio/)**  
  Learning designers grounded scripts in each course’s curriculum and evaluated language level and coherence. DuoRadio grew from 300 to more than 15,000 episodes in fewer than two quarters. Daily sessions rose from 500,000 to five million as more learners gained access.

- **[Qian Yang — fitting AI into a decision people already make](https://publications.ri.cmu.edu/unremarkable-ai-fitting-intelligent-decision-support-into-critical-clinical-decision-making-processes)**  
  Yang’s team embedded machine prognoses in slides clinicians already discussed together. The placement preserved the group’s chance to deliberate. Her [Cornell research group](https://designai.cis.cornell.edu/) studies how to design useful interactions when teams are still learning what their models can reliably do.

- **[Cecily Morrison — helping a blind child initiate a conversation](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/peoplelens-using-ai-to-support-social-interaction-between-children-who-are-blind-and-their-peers/)**  
  PeopleLens speaks a familiar person’s name from their direction when the wearer turns toward them. In [research with a blind child](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/social-sensemaking-with-ai-designing-an-open-ended-ai-experience-with-a-blind-child/), the team examined whether he could choose whom to approach. That social goal gives the design a more meaningful test than face recognition alone.

- **[Archana Prasad — reaching people in their own language and channel](https://www.help.gooey.ai/farmerchat)**  
  Prasad cofounded Gooey.AI, which built Farmer.CHAT with Digital Green. Farmers can ask questions by voice or text using familiar messaging channels. Her [design research](https://www.rca.ac.uk/research-innovation/research-degrees/research-students/archana-prasad/) also explores how communities can shape the use of their own knowledge in AI.

### Can people check an answer or challenge a decision?

- **[Microsoft Copilot — learning what makes an answer worth coming back for](https://outset.ai/resources/stories/how-microsoft-increased-copilot-retention-by-five-percent-with-outset)**  
  Christopher Monnier and colleagues asked customers to [compare answers to their own prompts](https://microsoft.design/articles/putting-the-user-back-into-ai-evaluation/) and explain what worked. Microsoft internally estimated roughly 5% higher retention following product changes. The public case leaves the time window and comparison method unspecified.

- **[Jason Spielman — keeping a claim and its source visible together](https://jasonspielman.com/notebooklm)**  
  NotebookLM’s early design lead shares the sketches behind its source, chat, and output panels. When someone checks a citation, they can keep the generated claim in view while reading the source. That small layout decision makes verification easier.

- **[Min Kyung Lee — letting affected people help write an algorithm’s rules](https://minlee.ischool.utexas.edu/materials/Publication/2019-CSCW-AI_Participation.pdf)**  
  Food donors, recipients, staff, and volunteers helped design WeBuildAI’s allocation rules. Replaying five months of data directed more donations to higher-poverty areas and reduced median travel from 6.15 to 5.5 miles. Lee’s [Stanford talk](https://hci.cs.stanford.edu/courses/cs547/speaker.php?date=2019-01-18) explains how stakeholders made the trade-offs.

- **[Michelle S. Lam — giving people the means to audit a model](https://hci.stanford.edu/publications/paper.php?id=470)**  
  With [Lam’s](https://michelle123lam.github.io/) IndieLabel prototype, 17 nontechnical participants tested where a toxicity model disagreed with their judgments. They surfaced veiled hate the model missed and reclaimed language it flagged. The method helps teams discover errors their existing test cases overlook.

- **[Saleema Amershi and collaborators — designing for the moment AI gets it wrong](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/)**  
  Their human–AI interaction guidelines cover what to communicate at first use, how users can recover from mistakes, and what happens as the system adapts. The guidance is useful when a team needs explicit acceptance criteria for failure and recovery.

### What should a customer delegate to an agent?

- **[Emily Campbell — defining what an agent must ask before it acts](https://www.shapeof.ai/)**  
  The Shape of AI catalogs patterns for consent, memory, and confirmation. Campbell’s [framework for AI experiences](https://shapeofai.substack.com/p/designing-beneath-the-surface-the) helps teams decide whether to change the interface, the information the model receives, or the actions it is allowed to take.

- **[Maggie Appleton — giving collaborators a say in what an agent changes](https://githubnext.com/projects/chopin/)**  
  Appleton’s [Language Model Sketchbook](https://maggieappleton.com/lm-sketchbook) imagined an editor that could challenge a writer’s claim. In Chopin, a research prototype built with Krzysztof Cieślak, several people can discuss a passage and ask an agent to revise it. The instruction and decision stay visible to the team.

- **[Raluca Budiu — studying the handoff between a person and an agent](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-vs-experience-design/)**  
  Budiu distinguishes a customer using an interface, an agent using it for them, and both working together. Each calls for a different usability test. A handoff is a good place to look for whether a person can interrupt or correct an action.

- **[Olivia Moore and Justine Moore — designing for a buyer represented by software](https://a16z.com/welcome-to-the-b2a-economy/)**  
  The Andreessen Horowitz authors examine how an agent might discover a product and purchase it for a customer. [Stripe and OpenAI’s commerce protocol](https://stripe.com/blog/developing-an-open-standard-for-agentic-commerce) makes the roles concrete by defining how the buyer, merchant, and agent participate in checkout.

- **[DDX — asking who will read, choose, or transact on a customer’s behalf](https://www.ddxconference.com/research/designing-for-the-machine-57-leaders)**  
  Of 57 leaders answering an open question about digital innovation, eight independently raised the changing role of agents. Firdosh Tangri, Harriet Swan, and Ivan Barriga describe different expectations for a product when software is also reading it.

- **[Tony Fadell — deciding whether an AI device earns a place in someone’s life](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/07/tony-fadell-on-why-the-first-wave-of-ai-gadgets-failed-and-what-comes-next/)**  
  Reflecting on Rabbit, Humane, and Limitless, Fadell asks which everyday problem justifies carrying another device. His second question is about trust. What would persuade someone to give a new assistant sensitive information or permission to act?

*Sources checked November 17, 2026.*
